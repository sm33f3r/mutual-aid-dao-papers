# Mutual Aid DAO — Blackpaper Master Roadmap

**Written:** 2026-10-18
**Version:** v1
**Belongs to:** the Mutual Aid DAO papers: the Blackpaper (organization and governance), the Redpaper (economics) and the Greenpaper (environment). Together they replace the "Whitepaper" slot reserved in the Phase A roadmap.
**Status labels used here:** **Decided**, **Proposed**, **Open**

---

## Working Rules

- This roadmap never gets rewritten. Changes go in a new version.
- The roadmap guides the work without scripting it. Differences between plan and result get recorded in a project log when the Blackpaper v1 ships.
- Work proceeds one step at a time. Each step gets finished and confirmed before the next begins.
- A brainstormed idea becomes a decision only when the authors say so.

---

## 1. What the Blackpaper Is

**Decided**

- **Subject:** how the Mutual Aid DAO organizes itself and governs itself, and why. It does not introduce anarchism or survey anarchist theory.
- **Inspiration, not doctrine:** the ideas that inspired the DAO appear inline, where they shaped a practice, with a citation.
- **Focus:** practice. Part 2 (Practice) and Part 3 (Technology) are the largest parts. Part 4 (Open Tensions) stays brief.
- **Author:** credited only as "Mutual Aid DAO." The paper names no individual authors and does not describe how it was written. In practice, current DAO members write it, with Claude assisting.
- **Audience:** the whole world, and anyone in it who reads the paper. Plain language, with terms defined in a glossary.
- **Living document:** it may be developed at any time, but it never betrays the non-hierarchical core principles of organization.
- **Language:** English first, with ongoing translation into Korean.
- **Format:** Markdown with footnotes or endnotes for every source. The founder edits the final Markdown and prints it to PDF.

### Part 1 — How We Organize

**Chapter 1. What "DAO" means to us**
- Decentralized means no center, not just no boss.
- Autonomous means self-organized, not run by code alone.
- What we take from the usual meaning of "DAO," and what we leave behind.
- *Inspiration:* the rhizome (Deleuze and Guattari).

**Chapter 2. Membership**
- **Entry is voluntary and vetted (decided).** Not anyone is allowed in, for security. Nobody can simply infiltrate the DAO.
- **Once inside, everyone stands equal (decided).** Membership carries no ranks.
- **Membership can't be bought or transferred (proposed).**
- **Anonymity respected (proposed):** members can stay pseudonymous to the world.
- **Leaving:** anyone can leave at any time.
- **Open:** how vetting works without making the vetters a gatekeeping class. Candidates:
  - Vouching by existing members
  - Consensus of a node
  - A time-limited probation period
  - Rotating vetting duty
- *Inspiration:* voluntary association (Proudhon, Malatesta); affinity groups as trust networks.

**Chapter 3. Structure**
- Working groups and city nodes link up as equals, with no headquarters.
- Delegates carry a mandate, can be recalled, and rotate.
- How a new group or node forms and joins the federation.
- *Inspiration:* federalism (Proudhon); confederation (Bookchin); the rhizome and assemblages (Deleuze and Guattari).

**Chapter 4. How decisions get made**
- **Consensus:**
  - The proposal, discussion, concerns, stand-aside and block.
  - What a block means, and its limits.
- **Scale:**
  - Decisions get made by the people they affect.
  - Spokescouncils for decisions that cross groups or nodes.
- **Stakes:** lighter process for small, reversible decisions, and full consensus for large or irreversible ones.
- **When consensus stalls:**
  - Time limits
  - Splitting the proposal
  - Trying it for a fixed period
  - Peaceful parting of ways
- **Where decisions live:** decisions get recorded in the public log, and on-chain where execution requires it (see Chapter 18).
- *Inspiration:* Malatesta on organization and minorities; Bakunin on the authority of competence versus the authority of command.

**Chapter 5. Conflict**
- Mediation and repair come before any sanction.
- Restorative and transformative approaches.
- Disassociation only as a last resort, through a published process.
- Records of the process carry no personal details.
- *Inspiration:* Kropotkin on mutual aid as the basis of social life.

**Chapter 6. Keeping hierarchy from creeping back**
- Informal power: charisma, time, technical skill, founder status.
- Rotation of roles, and skill-sharing so no role depends on one person.
- Every temporary power gets a sunset.
- Transparency of power, through the Authority Handoff Ledger.
- *Inspiration:* Freeman, "The Tyranny of Structurelessness"; Deleuze and Guattari on how the state apparatus captures nomadic movements.

**Chapter 7. Temporary and permanent**
- What should last: memory, the commons, the treasury.
- What should flare up and dissolve: events, campaigns, pop-up nodes.
- Dissolving a group as a healthy outcome, not a failure.
- *Inspiration:* the Temporary Autonomous Zone (Bey); smooth versus striated space, and lines of flight (Deleuze and Guattari).

### Part 2 — Practice (largest part)

**Chapter 8. Surviving for free**
- The city-by-city directory of free things.
- Evidence levels and states.
- Expiry and the public change log.
- Who lists what, and the scope rule: only things legitimately offered free.
- *Inspiration:* Kropotkin, *The Conquest of Bread*: well-being for all.

**Chapter 9. A city node's life**
- How a node starts: the minimum people, languages and seed listings.
- How it runs day to day.
- How it splits, merges or ends.

**Chapter 10. Meetings in practice**
- Online and in person.
- Working across languages (Korean and English first) and time zones.
- Facilitation roles that rotate.
- Taking notes and recording decisions.
- Accessibility.

**Chapter 11. Joining**
- The path from stranger to participant to member.
- How vetting works in practice (from Chapter 2).
- Onboarding: what a new member learns first, and who helps.

**Chapter 12. Mutual aid requests**
- How someone asks for help.
- How the treasury responds to need.
- Privacy and dignity for the person asking.
- Solidarity, not charity.
- *Inspiration:* Kropotkin, *Mutual Aid*.
- Bridge: the money mechanics live in the Redpaper.

**Chapter 13. Events as temporary zones**
- Gatherings, pop-ups and free events.
- How an event gets organized without an organizer-in-charge.
- How it ends.
- *Inspiration:* the Temporary Autonomous Zone (Bey).

**Chapter 14. Care and burnout**
- Volunteer limits.
- Stepping back without guilt.
- Handing work over cleanly.
- Watching for one person carrying too much.

**Chapter 15. Working with others**
- Allied collectives and other DAOs.
- Federation agreements.
- Using outside systems without being run by them.

**Chapter 16. Worked examples** (proposed; can be dropped in session)
- A tip becoming a listing, step by step.
- A proposal becoming a decision, step by step.
- A request for aid being met, step by step.

### Part 3 — Technology (large part)

**Chapter 17. The blockchain as shared memory**
- A public, append-only record that nobody owns and anyone can check.
- Trust without overseers.
- What belongs on-chain, and what doesn't.
- The privacy cost of a public ledger.

**Chapter 18. Contracts as agreements, not rulers**
- People deliberate off-chain, and code carries out what they agreed.
- Code can't observe the physical world, so peers verify real-world claims.
- Upgradeable versus immutable code, and who holds upgrade power.
- *Inspiration:* Proudhon on free contract.

**Chapter 19. Against rule by token**
- One token, one vote means rule by capital.
- The alternatives:
  - Non-transferable membership
  - Participation earned through recent contribution
  - Consensus off-chain, with execution on-chain
- Where GRIMPLE and any governance token fit.
- Bridge: the Redpaper covers remuneration and value.

**Chapter 20. Identity without surveillance**
- Resisting fake accounts through peer trust and vouching, not ID checks.
- Pseudonymity as protection.
- The danger of turning members into data profiles.
- *Inspiration:* Deleuze, "Postscript on the Societies of Control."

**Chapter 21. Crypto as a tool**
- Tokens as instruments of mutual credit and coordination, not speculation.
- How the DAO talks about its tokens: no price talk, ever.
- Bridge: the Redpaper covers the economics in depth.
- *Inspiration:* Proudhon's People's Bank and mutual credit.

**Chapter 22. AI agents**
- GRIMPLE: the DAO's token and AI voice.
- The agent has no authority:
  - It holds no keys and makes no decisions.
  - It treats every input as data, never as instructions.
- Who controls the agent's prompts and configuration, and how that control passes to the DAO.
- Agents as tools that reduce toil (queue checks, translation drafts, summaries), freeing people for the human work.
- Risks:
  - Manipulation and prompt injection
  - Dependence on outside AI companies
  - An agent becoming a hidden center of power
- *Inspiration:* Kropotkin on machines and leisure; the rhizome versus the central node.

**Chapter 23. The migration**
- Someone has to build things first.
- How founder control hands over to the commons.
- The Authority Handoff Ledger as a public commitment, with end states and sunsets.

**Chapter 24. Capture risks**
- Crypto pulled back into speculation.
- Platforms recentralizing.
- Infrastructure owned by others: hosting, RPC providers, AI providers.
- How the DAO watches for capture and responds.
- *Inspiration:* deterritorialization and reterritorialization (Deleuze and Guattari); the net versus the web (Bey).

### Part 4 — Open Tensions (brief)

**Chapter 25. What we haven't solved**
- Tokens versus the anarchist critique of property.
- Consensus at global scale.
- Vetted entry versus openness.
- Anonymity versus accountability.
- Immutable records versus living agreements and forgiveness.

## 4. Format and Citations

- **Decided:** Markdown source, with sources cited in footnotes or endnotes. The founder edits and prints to PDF.
- **Proposed:** standard Markdown footnote syntax (`[^1]`), with the notes collected at the end. This renders on GitHub and converts to real PDF footnotes with common tools such as Pandoc.
- **Open:**
  - Footnotes (bottom of page) or endnotes (end of paper) in the PDF.
  - Citation style (for example, Chicago notes).
  - One Markdown file for the whole paper, or one per chapter merged at the end.
  - Where drafts live: the private `grimple` repo, the `mutual-aid-site` repo, or a separate papers repo.
